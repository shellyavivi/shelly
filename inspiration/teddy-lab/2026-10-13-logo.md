**Decisions on open items (agent decides):**
- **Relationship to Build-A-Bear:** treated as an **endorsed sub-brand**. Teddy Lab has its own mark and can carry a small "by Build-A-Bear" line, but borrows none of the parent's visual language. It is a machine product, so it needs its own signature that can sit on hardware. If it is a fully separate company, nothing below changes except the endorsement line.
- **What "modern" means:** each direction sits at a different point on the scale the brief gives. *Proven* sits at the design-led toy end (Miffy, Lovevery). *Stand Out* sits at the lab/tool end (Braun, Teenage Engineering). *Concept* sits at the textile-process end (Woolmark, Miyake).
- **Mark type:** a different archetype for each direction (combination mark / grid lettermark / dynamic single-line mark).

**Visual vocabulary**
- **Core idea (primary form):** a visible process. A line being drawn, a seam closing, a construction grid left visible, a part shown before assembly.
- **Pillar 1, process & craft:** one stitch, one seam, a running-stitch dash, a pattern-cutting notch, a button-like dot. Use **one** at a time, never all.
- **Pillar 2, shared experience:** two parts that join (person + machine, two halves of a seam), an open form that closes, a mark that can be "finished" by the user.
- **Pillar 3, cute:** circles, round ears, short proportions, a low-set face. Kept geometric so it never turns into a mascot.
- **Pillars 4–5, modern & style:** flat colour, one weight, generous negative space, contemporary sans or mono type, a limited palette.
- **Tone, handfelt + professional:** warm off-whites and felt-like mid-tones rather than pure white. Monoline strokes with rounded caps (like thread), no gradients, no bevels.
- **Constraints (filter):** each mark must reduce to about 3 primitives at 16px, have no hairlines under 1.5px at 24px, embroider at about 25mm (minimum running-stitch length about 1.5–2mm), and work in one colour and reversed.

## Direction 1 — Proven: "Three Circles and a Button"

**Archetype:** **Combination mark.** A rounded lowercase wordmark plus a geometric bear-head symbol.
*Why:* this is the category's most legible structure. Kids read the symbol, parents read the name, and the
symbol can work alone as the app icon and the plush label. The symbol does the cute work, so the wordmark can stay calm and professional.

**Idea:** a bear head built from three circles (head + two ears). It carries **one** craft detail: a small
"button" dot on one ear, a nod to Steiff's 1904 button-in-ear. Next to it sits a soft, lowercase geometric wordmark, `teddy lab`.

**Category conventions used:** rounded geometric lowercase type, a simple character symbol, flat friendly colour, the
"provenance tag" idea from the plush world. They're useful because they're instantly legible to families, low-risk next to a
parent retail brand, and scale easily to signage and screens.

**Why it answers the brief:** *Level 1:* the button is a small mark of craft and authenticity ("this was made, and made properly"). *Level 2:* craft
(button/tag), cute (three circles), modern (strict geometry, flat colour). *Level 3:* fresh and professional through restraint. The handfelt
warmth comes from colour and the rounded type, not illustration.

### Mark anatomy
- Head circle diameter **D**. Ears are circles of **0.42D**, centres at ±40° from vertical, overlapping the head by about 0.12D.
  Fillet the two joins so the outline is one continuous shape.
- **One** button dot of 0.12D, sitting off-centre in the *right* ear only. This asymmetry gives a "wink" without drawing a face.
- Optional face: two dots only, placed low (below the horizontal centre) for "cute" proportions. Test with and without.
- Solid fill, no outline. The reversed version knocks out the button.
- Wordmark: lowercase `teddy lab`, one weight (medium/bold). Swap the tittle on nothing, keep it plain. The "o"-like counters
  (d, b, a) should share the head's roundness.

### Palette / type / form cues
- Warm off-white "felt" `#F6EFE4` · caramel/honey `#C98B4E` · cocoa ink `#2B2420` · one accent, tomato `#E5533D` (the button).
- Form: circles and fillets only. No straight lines in the symbol. Straight lines appear only in the type.

### Sketch prompts
1. Three overlapping circles traced into one filleted silhouette. Draw 6 ear angles (30°–50°) and 3 ear sizes. Pick the one that reads as "bear" rather than "mouse".
2. The same silhouette with the button dot in 4 positions (ear centre, ear edge, forehead, cheek). Check which one reads as "made with care" rather than "eye".
3. Lockups: symbol left of `teddy lab` (horizontal for machine fascia), symbol above (stacked for app/screen), and symbol alone inside a woven label rectangle.

### Small-size test
- **16px:** head + ears survive as one blob, and the button becomes 2px, which is borderline. Make a favicon variant that drops the button, or uses the
  button as a knockout. **One colour:** works naturally (solid shape). **Embroidery:** satin-stitch fill of the silhouette, French-knot or
  satin dot for the button. Very achievable.

### Cohesion note
All of these do softness through **subtraction**: Fisher-Price's 3 scallops, Bruna's removal, Weston's single volume, Arp's
sanded curve, Tezuka's single loop. They share warm colour, round geometry and a quiet sans voice.

### Risks
- **Generic:** "bear head from circles" is the most common bear logo on stock sites. It will only stand out if the proportions and the button
  are drawn with real care. Benchmark against the stock examples rather than ignore them.
- **Mouse confusion:** big round ears read as Mickey. Keep the ears small and set wider.
- **Build-A-Bear adjacency:** the parent also uses a bear and friendly type. The button and the lowercase calm must separate them.

## Direction 2 — Stand Out: "Lab Spec"

**Archetype:** **Lettermark / monogram on a construction grid** (`tl`), with a mono wordmark.
*Why:* the machine is the product. A grid-built monogram can be stamped, engraved or lit on hardware, and it reads as a
**tool you trust**, which is what parents need to hear. The bear is implied, never drawn.

**Convention broken:** **cute-toy softness.** Rounded bubbly type, multicolour palettes and drawn characters give way to an
engineered mono wordmark, a strict grid, a single signal colour, and **no bear illustration at all**. The only cute
gesture: two rounded grid modules at the top of the monogram that read as ears.

**Idea:** a `t` and an `l` built from the same square module on a 5×5 grid. The two top terminals get a full radius, so the
monogram's silhouette is a bear head seen as a spec drawing. The wordmark is set in a monospaced grotesk, like a part number on a machine.

**Why it answers the brief:** *Level 1:* "craft you can watch" becomes **precision you can watch**. The grid is left visible, like
exposed mechanics. *Level 2:* process (construction lines are part of the brand), modern, style. Cute is limited to the two ears
on purpose. *Level 3:* professional and fresh. Handfelt comes from colour and the soft radius, not from texture.

### Hero reference
**MIT Media Lab identity · Pentagram (Michael Bierut, Aron Fay) · 2014.** Built on the **7×7 grid** of Richard The's earlier
anniversary logo. The grid generates an `ML` monogram and a family of glyphs for 23 research groups.
**Take:** **one grid, many glyphs.** The `tl` monogram and the machine's status glyphs (stuffing, stitching, done) can come from
the same module, so the logo is the first symbol in a system. Links:
[Pentagram news](https://www.pentagram.com/news/mit-media-lab) · [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/pentagram-mit) ·
[designboom](https://www.designboom.com/design/the-new-mit-media-lab-identity-by-pentagram/) · [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/?p=579622)

### Mark anatomy
- 5×5 square module grid. `t` = 1-module stem + 3-module crossbar. `l` = 1-module stem shifted one module right and **raised one
  module above the cap line** (Braun's raised A).
- The two top terminals (the `t` crossbar's left end and the `l` top) get a **full radius of 0.5 module**. Together they make the ear silhouette.
  All other corners stay sharp.
- Stroke = 1 module. Counters ≥ 1 module, so it holds at 16px on a 5px-module pixel grid.
- Optional construction lines (hairline, 1/8 module) shown only on large formats like the machine fascia and packaging, never on small ones.
- Wordmark `TEDDY LAB` or `teddy—lab` in mono, letterspaced 0. A part-number style suffix (`TL-01`) for each machine unit.

### Type direction
- **ABC Diatype Mono** (Dinamo; Johannes Breyer, Fabian Harb). Swiss neo-grotesque, with a name taken from pre-digital

- **LL Brown Mono** (Lineto) as an alternative with rounder geometry. [lineto.com/typefaces/brown-mono](https://lineto.com/typefaces/brown-mono)
- Classification: **monospaced grotesk** for the wordmark and UI. The monogram is drawn, not typeset.

### Palette / type / form cues
- Off-white "lab bench" `#F2F0EB` · graphite `#1E1E1E` · aluminium grey `#B9BCBF` · **one signal colour: honey/safety yellow `#FFC21A`**.
  It reads as both "bear/honey" and "industrial safety", and avoids TE orange and Build-A-Bear's colours.
- Form: squares, 90° angles, exactly two full radii.

### Sketch prompts
1. Draw `tl` on 4×4, 5×5 and 7×7 grids. Pick the smallest grid on which the two ear radii still read as ears and not as random rounding.
2. Same monogram, raised `l` vs. level `l`, with ears on both letters vs. one. Check which version shows a bear head in silhouette when filled solid.
3. Machine status glyph set from the same grid (empty, stuffing, stitching, heart inserted, done). Check that the monogram looks like the first member of the family.

### Small-size test
- **16px:** with a 5×5 grid on a 15px canvas (3px modules), strokes and counters are 3px. That's crisp, and the radii render as 1px
  corner cuts. Ears still read. **One colour:** native. **Embroidery:** fine as satin columns, and the square geometry stitches cleanly.
  Ears need a minimum of about 3mm per module.

### Cohesion note
Every reference treats **construction as content**: MIT's grid, Aicher's 45/90 rules, Braun's compass-and-ruler, the Bechers' typology,
Pompidou's exposed services, LeWitt's instructions. One cool-neutral palette, one mono voice, one hot accent.

### Risks
- **Too cold for parents of 5-year-olds.** If the ears don't read, it becomes a fintech logo. Test with kids.
- **TE pastiche.** Mono type + one bright colour is now a trend. The ears and the honey colour must make it Teddy Lab's own.
- Construction lines can turn into "too detailed". Keep them to large formats only.

## Direction 3 — Concept: "One Thread"

**Concept:** *The logo is the needle's path.* A single continuous line, with no lifts, draws the bear and closes on itself
like a finished seam. On screens it's drawn live, at the speed the machine actually stitches.
**Rationale (one sentence):** if the promise is "craft you can watch", the mark should *be* a piece of craft you watch happen:
one thread, entering, travelling and tying off.

**Archetype:** **Dynamic / generative mark** (single-stroke pictorial + single-stroke logotype).
*Why:* the machine and the logo can share one file. The same vector path is the embroidery program on the plush label, the
progress animation on the machine screen, and the static logo. The "shared experience" pillar comes in because the customer
presses the button that sends the thread. The person starts the line and the machine finishes it.

**Convention broken (secondary):** logos are static and finished. This one is a process with a start and an end.

**Why it answers the brief:** *Level 1:* literally process made visible. *Level 2:* process & craft (stitch), shared experience
(the person starts, the machine finishes), cute (bear outline), modern (monoline, generative). *Level 3:* handfelt (thread, running
stitch) and professional (one weight, one colour, engineering logic). It also guards against **"too detailed"** by construction: one line can't get busy.

### Hero reference
**Woolmark · Franco Grignani (attribution contested; officially credited to "Francesco Saroglia") · 1964.**
Rediscovered negatives show it was first built from **three copies of a nine-striped band**. It's often read as a skein of wool or a Möbius strip.
Creative Review readers voted it best logo of all time (2011).
**Take:** a textile mark made of **one continuous ribbon** with no illustration of a sheep or fabric. The material's *behaviour* (a skein) is
the image. Teddy Lab should show thread's behaviour, not a needle icon. Links:
[francogrignani.info](https://www.francogrignani.info/the-quite-strange-story-of-the-best-logo-ever/) ·
[Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/woolmark) · [AGI](https://a-g-i.org/design/woolmark/) ·
[Estorick Collection](https://estorickcollection.com/exhibitions/franco-grignani-art-as-design-1950-1990)
Visual: not checked.

  **Take:** the thread as a structural and graphic unit. Line, crossing and grid come *from* the material. Use it to justify a
  running-stitch dash (dash : gap = 2 : 1) as the brand's secondary line style.
- **Claes Oldenburg · *Soft Toilet* · 1966 · Whitney** · [whitney.org/collection/works/425](https://whitney.org/collection/works/425).
  **Take:** a rigid object remade **hand-sewn and stuffed**, sagging under gravity. Use this for the "soft" version of the logotype in motion:
  letters that relax slightly once the stitch ties off, as if they've been stuffed.

### Mark anatomy
- **One open path** with a start (needle in) and an end (tie-off). It traces head → left ear → over the crown → right ear → down to the
  chin, crossing itself once. The crossing is the "knot" and the only point of density.
- Monoline stroke with **round caps and round joins** (thread). Stroke weight = 8% of mark height at app-icon size, 2px at 24px.
- **Gap = handmade proof:** leave one small opening in the seam (the stuffing hole). On screen it closes when the customer's bear is done.
  This is the "shared experience" moment.
- Logotype: `teddy lab` written as **one connected stroke path** (a Hershey-style single-stroke skeleton, customised). The thread
  leaves the bear's tie-off and runs straight into the `t`.
- Secondary style: the same path rendered as a **running stitch** (dashes, 2:1) for packaging and the pattern-piece layouts.

### Type direction
- **Custom single-stroke logotype** modelled on **Hershey Simplex / Hershey Sans** (stroke-defined, machine-native).
  [Hershey Text for Inkscape](https://www.evilmadscientist.com/2011/hershey-text-an-Inkscape-extension-for-engraving-fonts) for fast prototyping.
- For text alongside it, use a quiet grotesk with no character of its own so the thread stays the only voice. *(open, to be picked in phase 2. Avoid GT Maru and LL Brown so this stays separate from Direction 1.)*
- Classification: **monoline stroke / engraving font** for the logo, neutral grotesk for UI.

### Palette / type / form cues
- Undyed "calico" `#EFE6D8` · thread red `#C8372D` (the single thread colour) · denim/indigo ink `#26324A` for text · unbleached white for plush labels.
- Form: one line, round caps, one crossing, one gap. No fills anywhere.

### Sketch prompts
1. Without lifting the pen, draw a bear head in **one stroke** 20 times in 10 seconds each. Keep the 3 that close most naturally. Then rebuild
   them in vector with ≤ 8 anchor points.
2. Join the best path to a single-stroke `teddy lab`. Try the thread leaving from the chin, from an ear, and from the "stuffing gap".
3. Storyboard the 4-second screen animation: needle in → path drawn at constant speed → gap left open → (bear complete) gap closes →
   a small tie-off dot. Then test the same path as an actual embroidery file at 25mm.

### Small-size test
- **16px:** the full path is too fine. Use a **fixed favicon variant**: a thicker stroke (2px at 16px) and a simplified path (ears + head only, no
  crossing). It still reads as one loop. **One colour:** native (it is one line). **Embroidery:** ideal. It's a single running or
  triple-run stitch path with no jumps, which is cheaper and cleaner than any fill. **Risk at small size:** a monoline outline can disappear
  on busy photos. Always place it on a solid colour field.

### Cohesion note
Every reference is about **the line as the material's own behaviour**: Woolmark's ribbon, Whitney's pulse line, Hershey's stroke paths,
A-POC's woven seam outlines, Penn's flat silhouettes, Otto's tension lines, Albers' thread, Oldenburg's sewn softness. The palette is warm
undyed textile, the voice is monoline, and everything is textured, nothing glossy.

### Risks
- **Single-line art is a cliché** (wedding monograms, "one-line" tattoos). The difference has to be the machine logic: the gap, the
  real embroidery path, the live drawing. Without those it's just decoration.
- An outline bear can look thin or fragile next to a parent brand. The pairing needs testing.
- Dynamic marks need production discipline (a motion spec, a fixed static key frame).
